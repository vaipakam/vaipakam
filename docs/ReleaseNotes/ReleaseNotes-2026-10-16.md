Four pull requests with release notes merged on this day, two of them the last
two of the three that close out the VPFI recycling programme's build. The second

## Thread — the mesh watcher's two blind spots closed: a late-delivered release resolves, and Base's delivered share is checked against the mirror (#1452, #1461)

**#1461 — a released remittance that later landed stayed counted as stranded backing.** Releasing a remittance whose message seems never to execute records its recycled share as backing stranded in transit. The watcher's coverage check counts that as backing that exists. Nothing reduced it when the message executed after all, so the allowance overstated in-transit backing by the late-delivered amount and could hide a real shortfall of that size.

Each reservation now keeps the share its own release recorded. When the destination acknowledges that it credited a released remittance after all, that share is resolved once. It goes into the figure the allowance already nets out, the same figure a recovery settlement or a recorded terminal loss feeds. Those two routes take their share off the same record first, so one remittance is never resolved twice.

A delivery the destination held in quarantine instead of crediting resolves nothing on acknowledgement, and neither does a credit reported after such a hold. Its value is still the platform's to recover or write off, and the recovery or the loss record resolves it. The two sides are exclusive by one rule: recovery and loss records are refused once a credit stands unchallenged, and a destination cannot report holding a delivery after reporting a credit for it.

The keeper's ack pass now also acknowledges a released remittance whose delivery has landed, covering the case where nobody would otherwise pay for the acknowledgement. It stops once the destination's classification is recorded. A late delivery nobody acknowledges still stays counted until somebody does. Nothing that moves value reads this figure; it governs an alert, not a payout.

**#1452 — a delivery booked as absorption instead of relocated custody went unseen.** When a mirror receives a remittance, it must book the recycled share as relocated custody, not as its own absorption. A delivery credited the ordinary way instead keeps every relation between the mirror's own figures true. The mirror then reports the canonical chain's top-up as its own absorption, and the canonical chain offers it back as that mirror's funding. Only a record the mirror does not write can catch that.

The canonical chain now records, per destination, the recycled share of every remittance the destination acknowledged crediting, once each, and publishes it. What it records is the share the destination actually relocated: on a short delivery, where a token fee reduces what lands, the destination scales the recycled share down to what arrived, and the canonical chain books that same scaled figure, so the two sides agree and a short delivery never reads as a shortfall. The figure kept apart for an operator's forced finalization stays the declared share, because a forced finalization carries no delivery figure. An acknowledgement that the delivery is held or still provisional books nothing, since the mirror relocates custody only when it credits; a released remittance credited late is booked. Forced finalizations are counted apart because they are evidenced rather than delivered; one that the delivery's own acknowledgement later confirms moves over to the acknowledged figure. The watcher pages when that figure exceeds the mirror's relocated custody. The acknowledgement always follows the delivery's credit, so the canonical chain's figure trails the mirror's; leading it means a misbooked arrival. When the figure cannot be read, the check is skipped and the gap is named.

**History on an upgraded chain (#2489 review).** Both records above — the per-remittance stranded share and the per-destination booked share — were added after remittances had already been released and finalized on the deployed chains, so a chain refreshed in place starts with every earlier remittance unrecorded: a late-landing release would have resolved nothing (its stranded allowance staying inflated for good), and the booked share would have undercounted every pre-upgrade delivery while the mirror's relocated custody held them all — a cushion exactly the size of that history, inside which a misbooked arrival would go unseen. Both facts are now derived from what each remittance record already holds — its status, its declared shares, its acknowledgement flags, and what the recovery ceremony and loss record already resolved for it — never from a figure an operator supplies. They are written the moment they are needed (a late acknowledgement, a ceremony) and, for the whole history at once, by a permissionless, repeatable walk anyone may run over the remittance ids in chunks; a second pass writes nothing. A view reports how much history is still unrecorded, and the watcher reads the booked share as unknown — naming the walk to run — until that reads zero over the whole history, rather than comparing an undercount. History is booked APART from the exact figure, at the declared share: the remittance record keeps no received figure (only the acknowledgement announcement carried it), so for a pre-upgrade delivery that arrived short the declared share overstates what the destination relocated, and folding it into the exact figure would have raised a false alarm. The exact figure therefore counts only acknowledgements the platform booked as they arrived. The history figure holds every earlier remittance the record marks as consumed — which includes an operator's forced finalization, because the forced path left the same mark and the record cannot tell the two apart; the destination's receipt can (a forced remittance never arrived, so the destination holds no delivery for it), and the reconstruction below counts such a remittance as having relocated nothing. For the history, the watcher reconstructs what the destination actually relocated by joining the canonical chain's list of history-booked remittances (each with the split it was sent with) against the destination's own receipt records (each with the amount that actually arrived) — so the hard invariant compares the exact figure plus that reconstructed history against the destination's relocated custody, and a delivery made after the upgrade cannot hide inside the custody relocated before it. Only where a destination holds no receipt for a history remittance does the watcher fall back to comparing the declared-share figure as an advisory, saying how many receipts were missing. A released remittance that the destination nevertheless credited later is booked the same way, as the live late-acknowledgement path books it — otherwise every such pre-upgrade delivery would have stayed exactly the cushion this closes. The watcher reads the whole history in pages each tick, so a long-lived deployment is judged on whether anything is unrecorded, never on its length alone.

**History.** On a fresh deployment there are no older releases, so no historical figure needs seeding.

Closes #1452. Closes #1461. Refs #1349, #1222.

## Thread — a loan's activity timeline shows when its rewards were forfeited (#1566)

**Problem.** The sanctions forfeit added an event that belongs to a specific loan: the settlement of a forfeited reward entry. The indexer recorded it but did not attach it to its loan, so a loan's activity timeline could never show it. Its own coverage test, which requires every loan-referencing event to be attached or deliberately left out with a reason, had been failing since the forfeit landed.

**Fix.** The settlement now attaches to its loan and appears on its timeline, with its own label in English and in the nine translated languages. The sanctions stamp on a reward entry is deliberately kept off every user-facing surface, in line with the rule that user-facing copy never mentions sanctions; the forfeit itself still shows, through its settlement. Refs #1566.

## Thread — a flagged wallet is paid nothing until revoked; what it earned while flagged is forfeited, what it earned before is held for it (#1566)

**Problem.** A wallet flagged by the sanctions oracle could not claim its interaction reward, and its entries' removal clock stopped while it was flagged. Nothing else ever moved those entries, so they stood for as long as the flag did. That froze the reward indefinitely, and it also held back the release of every old-wire delivery that listed one of those days, which the delivered-bound design recorded as an open owner question. Separately, the staged claim path that shipped in the staging change (#2308) forfeited a claimant's undelivered share on a single, fail-open oracle read.

**The rule (owner 2026-10-14, revising 2026-10-02).** After a wallet is flagged, no reward is paid to it until the flag is revoked. Reward it earned while flagged is forfeited, never delivered. Reward it earned before the flag is held for it and paid once the flag is revoked. Value already in the wallet's vault stays frozen. Loan proceeds are unchanged. The earlier rule of 2026-10-02 forfeited everything undelivered once a flag was confirmed, earlier earnings included; the owner narrowed it so a wallet keeps what it earned before it was flagged.

**When a flag counts.** One classifier decides, for any wallet, whether its reward is delivered, withheld, or settled under the rule. A flag counts only once it is *confirmed*: the oracle reports the wallet flagged now; the platform's committed flag has not been cleared by a clean read since; the classifier's own evidence was recorded under the same oracle; and the evidence forms a chain of flagged observations spanning a three-day window, with no gap longer than one day between observations. Both bounds are fixed. A first flag, a gap over one day, a pause or an unset oracle lasting longer than that, or a change of oracle all start the window again. A flag revoked before its chain confirms forfeits nothing, and what was withheld meanwhile is paid. An unset or unreachable oracle never forfeits anything. A wallet that declared a flagged stuck-token recovery source is treated the same way, as it already was everywhere else. Anyone may record an observation, and every observation is an authoritative oracle read, so no caller can forge or break another wallet's chain.

**"Earned while flagged" is counted by reward day.** A confirmed chain covers the days from its first flagged observation through its last. When such a chain ends, because a clean read clears it or a new chain starts over it, those days are recorded and announced. A day settled later is still forfeited, including by the wallet's own claim after the flag was revoked. Days after the last flagged observation are not counted, so time nobody observed is given the benefit of the doubt. A chain that ended before the rewards programme launched names no day at all — there was no reward day to forfeit, and an earlier revision would have charged the programme's first day to a wallet that was clean by then; a chain still open at the launch counts from that first day. Windows that touch or overlap are kept as one record, and settling a day costs the same however long a wallet's listing history is. The rule applies on every settlement path: the ordinary day-by-day claim, a staged day, and the older pre-cutover reward (the per-day window and an entry's pre-cutover slice, which is split by day).

**Settling a flagged wallet.** Once a flag is confirmed, anyone may run a permissionless settlement for that wallet. It first records an observation, so a keeper running it is also the chain's heartbeat. It then settles the wallet's reward exactly as the wallet's own claim would, with the same pricing, caps and bounds. The days earned while flagged go into the recycle bucket as forfeited reward. The rest is set apart for the wallet in a held balance of its own, in a custody row of its own, and every amount is announced with the wallet. Nothing reaches the wallet, and the settlement records no claim for it. Settling ends the reward obligation the way any claim does, so a flag that is never lifted holds back no day, no delivery release, no staged record and no custody step. The held balance therefore needs no deadline. The wallet's first claim after the flag is revoked pays its whole held balance together with anything else it is owed, to the venue that claim names. The wallet can also take the held balance alone, through a new claim that pays only that balance and refuses a wallet still barred. So if the ordinary claim cannot complete for another reason, such as an exhausted pool, the held balance is still reachable. The app's Claim Center shows the set-aside balance and offers that claim whenever it cannot preview the wallet's ordinary claim. The balance was charged to the reward pool and the delivered ledger when it was set apart, so paying it out charges neither again. No caller can choose the held-apart destination for itself.

**Removed.** The earlier design's per-entry forfeit mark, its eligibility rule and read view, and the owner forfeit sweep are gone; the settlement replaces them. The per-loan forfeit sweep still announces each settled chunk of a forfeited entry.

**Claims, clocks and keepers.** The claim refuses any *barred* owner: one flagged now, and also one carrying a committed flag (the #1123 marker) while the oracle is unreachable. The claim's check is itself a committed observation, the same one anyone may record. A clean reading at a claim therefore commits the clear and ends any stale chain, so a later flag starts a new confirmation window. Before, the check only read the oracle, and a chain the clean reading should have ended could carry on and confirm from evidence that predated the clear. The removal clock pauses on the same barred test, so the clock and the claim gate always agree. A keeper carrying a committed flag is skipped for its keeper reward during an outage. New read views show a wallet's confirmation status, its recorded windows, and its held balance (with the total held for every wallet). Before custody activation the held reward rests in the Diamond's own balance and is never counted as backing for new payouts; activation requires its custody row to hold exactly the total held.

**A position that changes hands keeps its reward with the wallet that earned it.** When a loan closes, its reward entry is normally re-pointed to whoever holds the position then. Because the flagged-day rule is applied to the wallet an entry belongs to, that move would re-price every earned day against the new holder — a confirmed-flagged owner could be delisted, hand the position to a clean wallet and have the flagged days paid; a clean owner's days could be forfeited under a flagged recipient's history. So when either wallet carries sanctions history the entry is never re-priced against the holder. At a loan close, where nothing remains to accrue, it is kept whole with its earner and announced as kept; the earner settles it under their own rule and the holder receives nothing from it. When a position is consolidated while its loan is still open, the entry is SPLIT at that day: the days earned so far close with the earner as a claimable entry of theirs, and the days from the next one accrue to the new holder as a fresh entry of theirs — announced as split, with the loan's reward pointer on the new entry. A transfer between two wallets with no history moves the entry intact as before. Both re-points now run on the interaction-rewards facet behind an internal call, so that facet joins the refresh set below.

**The claim page and a set-aside balance.** While a set-aside balance is ready to claim, the claim page does not describe the wallet's claims as empty.

**A staged claim day.** A staged claim's last page records an observation first. A day the claimant earned while a confirmed flag stood is forfeited at once, whatever the claimant's status now. A day earned before a confirmed flag is set apart for the claimant. With an unconfirmed flag, or with a committed flag while the oracle is unreachable, the page pays and retires nothing and is retried later. A staged day the settlement itself opened keeps the set-aside destination: its payout is set apart for the owner even if the flag has been lifted by the time the page pays, and the owner then takes it with the set-aside claim — nothing is lost, one more transaction. While a page waits, its record still counts as encumbering staging, which blocks reward-custody activation and reward-role changes. The wait is bounded by the three-day window when someone observes the wallet at least daily, and by the outage otherwise. A last page with no user share never waits, and expiry, forfeit and legacy records are never screened.

**Accounting fix.** Two headroom subtractions in the existing expiry sweeps could underflow when a chunk reported more than the remaining headroom. They are now floored at zero.

**Refresh together.** These facets must be refreshed together on every chain: the claim facet, the claim walk facet, the staging settlement facet, the staging domain facet, the horizon sweep facet, the custody facet, the expiry sweep's walk facet, the interaction-rewards lens (it carries the sanctions status reads and the transferability check the sweeps call) and the interaction-rewards facet (every loan close reaches its re-point entry). The complete in-place refresh is the vehicle — it refreshes every facet and removes retired entries from the loupe — and a curated refresh must carry this whole set. The custody cutover version advances (from 4 to 5) because the claim now reaches the custody facet through a new entry for the held-apart balance, so a cut that refreshed the claim facet but not the custody facet cannot be certified as complete; activation requires the stamp with every one of these facets refreshed. A wallet with nothing set apart makes no such call, so for it an ordinary claim never depends on that refresh. The claim body now settles the pre-cutover slices itself and walks only the armed days on the claim walk facet, whose entry changed shape. The staging domain facet's slice settle now returns the part a wallet earned while flagged. The horizon sweep facet hosts the evidence recorder and the per-day window check that the claim and staging paths reach. The custody facet hosts the set-apart and release moves. The expiry walk facet no longer screens sanctions itself, because the screen runs in the horizon sweep facet in front of it. The full in-place refresh covers all of them. The staging records' read views also move, with the same selectors, from the epoch view facet to the staging domain facet, because the view facet sat a few hundred bytes under the contract size limit. Storage gains appended fields: the per-wallet evidence record, the recorded windows, and the held balances with their total.

**Limits, stated.** Without a caller observing at least daily, a flagged owner's reward is only withheld. The keeper's sanctions-hold observer (#2450) is meant to be that caller; it ships separately (#2496), and until it does no flag is confirmed unless someone records the observations, so a flagged owner's reward is withheld rather than forfeited. The oracle setting carries a delayed reward-forfeit power: an oracle that flags a wallet forfeits what it earns while flagged once three days of observations confirm it. Timelock and pause are the mitigation, and unsetting the oracle stops every forfeit and pays instead. These cases stay frozen rather than forfeited or set apart: a flagged lender's entries on a loan awaiting its fallback resolution, the rewards of a principal behind an aggregator adapter, and vault reward VPFI, whose withdrawal stays fail-open during an outage as before. The borrower's fee rebate is loan value and is unchanged. A delisting shorter than one day that nobody observes can sit inside a confirmed chain; that is the same sampling limit as the expiry heartbeat's. Refs #1566.

**Spec:** TokenomicsTechSpec (the claim-horizon rules, the reward rule, the staging change) and ProjectDetailsREADME (the freeze-not-seize posture, scoped to loan value, with the reward rule). Design: Vpfi1566CanonicalDeliveredBoundDesign, the dated 2026-10-14 revision of the sanctioned-owner resolution.

**Custody activation.** Where reward custody is moved to its holder, the activation ceremony reads the set-aside total and funds or relocates its custody row exactly as it does the others — an explicit answer per row, refused up front when one is missing — so activation cannot be reached with the set-aside row unfunded.

## Thread — a mirror day that owes fresh funding waits for its own proven delivery (#1944)

**Problem.** On a mirror chain, the canonical chain's day broadcast is what opens that day's claims, and the budget remittance that funds the day travels as a separate message. Either can arrive first, and the broadcast is permissionless — anyone paying the transport fee can send a finalized day. A broadcast that landed before its budget opened claims against funding that had not arrived. The original bridge design treated that as a harmless "revert and retry". It was not: the mirror's balance also holds value that belongs to other owners, borrower collateral among it, so an early claim could be paid out of it. The only defence was the governance runbook's containment procedure — keep the reward messenger paused for the whole mesh — which could not be scoped to one mirror.

**What changed.** A mirror can now hold any day that owes it fresh funding until a budget delivery proven to list that day has landed there, whichever of the two messages arrives first. Until then the chain's reward cursor stops before the day, and every settlement path defers and moves no value: the claim, the windowed claim, per-loan entry settlement, both expiry sweeps, staging, domain preparation and the previews. A claim with nothing else to pay is refused with an error that names the day, so it is no longer reported as "nothing to claim". Whether a day owes fresh funding is read from what the canonical chain itself stamped and reported for that chain and day. So a day the canonical chain sends nothing for never waits: a destination excluded from the day, a side with no interest on that chain, an armed side whose reported liability is zero, or a side funded only from recycled value. Anyone may prove a delivery. A delivery that already carries indexed transport epochs counts as soon as that day's chunk is indexed. Any other budget delivery is proven by presenting its day list, or one rooted chunk of it, against the commitment it arrived with. Nothing is written per day when a delivery arrives, so the arrival keeps its existing gas budget. A proof records which delivery first listed the day and emits an event. It is provenance only and moves no value. A delivery already disposed of cannot prove a day; a delivery for an older custody era counts like any other, since PR 1's era revision no delivery is held back.

**Ships dark.** The gate is a one-shot, mirror-only switch that an administrator sets to the first day it binds. Until it is set, the pricing order is exactly what it was. Arm it on a mirror only once the keeper's delivery-proof pass runs there. That pass ships separately (#2496); until it does, the gate stays dark. A mirror whose custody era has moved may arm it too: since the custody-era revision every remittance carries the destination's own era and a retired era's delivery opens its epoch, so nothing is held inert. The switch refuses a first day that the reward cursor has already passed, because those days were priced without the gate. New read views give operators a day's delivery state and a delivery's proof state. One structural part is not behind the switch: the expiry sweep now runs its transfer test through the interaction-rewards read facet, so the reward facets must be refreshed together on every chain; a partial refresh stops expiry sweeps until that read facet is updated.

**Not dark — a pricing fix that applies on every mirror now.** A mirror now prices every day the canonical chain stamped from that stamp, whether the day is armed or not. Before, an unarmed (pre-cutover) day was priced from the fixed schedule. For an included day the two figures are the same, so nothing changes there. They differ in two cases, and in both the old pricing was wrong. First, a day the mirror was excluded from now pays zero on that mirror instead of the schedule. Second, a day stamped at a reduced floor because the fresh allocation was running out now pays that floor instead of the schedule. In both cases the mirror used to pay against funding the day never received. The canonical chain now remits an unarmed day from that same stamped figure instead of the schedule, so what a mirror is sent and what it pays agree on a reduced-floor day too. Before, the canonical chain sent the schedule's share there, more than the mirror pays, and when that share was larger than what was left of the pool the whole remittance was refused while a gated mirror waited for the day. The canonical chain's own pricing is unchanged. Because this fix is not behind the switch, the reward suites that deliver real broadcasts to a mirror are re-run rather than assumed green.

**Every delivery stays provable (#2489 review).** The canonical chain now records, on each remittance, which of the days it closed were actually funded, and keeps that record for good. The day list a rooted delivery commits to is rebuilt from it. Before, the list was rebuilt from the per-day remittance marks, which a release deletes and a later remittance of the same days rewrites. A delivery that executed after its remittance was released could then never be indexed or proven, and the days it funded stayed gated. Separately, a day crowded with newer deliveries no longer stops an older one from being indexed: a new read view lists a day's late arrivals in order, so whoever indexes the older delivery (the keeper, once its pass ships) can name, with the day list, the exact place where it belongs. The contract checks that place, so a wrong one is refused and never mis-orders anything.

**Limits, stated.** With the gate armed, availability depends on proofs. Every included day waits for its remittance and its proof, and armed days also wait for the chain's commitment report. A stalled keeper therefore produces named deferrals, never a loss. There is deliberately no administrative release on the mirror. A day whose delivery can never execute is repaired on the canonical chain by releasing the reservation, which needs seven days at the earliest, and remitting again. A change of the mirror's reward role after arming is safe: the canonical chain stamps the era each delivery will land in, and a delivery for a retired era still opens its epoch there. These cases stay outside the gate: days applied only by the legacy broadcast that carries no stamp; days before the gate's first day; any mirror whose gate is dark (the runbook's containment still applies to it); a late inclusion backfill on the canonical chain; and a delivery that has landed but cannot be drawn yet because it is not whole and attested, which the delivered bound governs. The mirror's rule is kept in step with the canonical chain's send rule through stored figures rather than shared code, and a mesh test pins that the two agree. Refs #1944 (consequence 1. The remedy, proving the day's own delivery, is new relative to the menu recorded on the issue and is recorded for the owner's ratification).

**Spec:** TokenomicsTechSpec §4a, "Reward pool funding on mirrors" and the claim behaviour list. Operator steps: GovernanceRunbook (M7 propagation), CcipCutoverRunbook (era rotation).

## Thread — a signed-offer maker can now opt into the Full tariff on the order they sign (#1949)

**Problem.** The Full tariff opt-in was reachable for standing offers (the creator sets it on their open offer after posting) and for acceptors (on the acceptance they sign), but not for a maker who posts a gasless, signed order: that order is materialized and accepted in one transaction, so there is no moment between posting and acceptance to set anything. A signed maker therefore always resolved as non-Full — safe, nothing was mis-charged — but the Full absorption channel was unreachable from the order book. The matched-fill half of this gap closed under the earlier card; this was its untracked remainder.

**What changed.** The signed order now carries the maker's Full opt-in — the opt-in flag, the ceiling on the maker's tariff and whether a downgrade is permitted — as part of the artifact the maker signs, so it travels with the signature. When a fill materializes the order (a direct fill, a wallet-backed fill, or a keeper matching a slice of it), the opt-in is applied to the materialized offer with the same fields and the same event the standing-offer setter uses, and the acceptance charges the maker's tariff and stamps Full exactly as it would for a standing offer: the tariff a party pays does not depend on the venue that filled their offer. A Full opt-in without a ceiling is refused before any value moves, on every fill path, as the setter refuses it. A slice filled by a keeper carries its share of the signed ceiling counted cumulatively: each slice takes the cumulative share at the end of its fill less the cumulative share before it, rounded up. So the slices' ceilings together never exceed the signed ceiling, and equal it once the order is fully filled. Rounding each slice up on its own would have let a keeper split an order into minimum fills that together authorized more than the maker signed, by one unit per slice. A slice's share comes out zero only when the signed ceiling is below one unit per slice, and such a slice cannot take Full. Makers who do not opt in are unchanged.

**A breaking signature shape.** Because the signed order gained fields, orders signed under the previous shape no longer verify. Pre-live, this is a book reset: the connected app, the indexer's order book and its validation, and the parity tests all moved to the new shape in the same change, and any order posted under the old shape must be re-signed. The indexer no longer serves such an order in the book: the contract can never accept it, and an app replaying it would have no value for the new fields. A partial fill's share of the maker's ceiling is computed at full width, so a very large signed ceiling no longer makes every partial fill fail. The order ticket offers the maker the control in its sign-only mode, while the feature is enabled. It quotes the tariff on the order's full size and pre-fills the ceiling from that quote. It says the choice is signed into the order and that each fill is charged its own tariff within its share of the ceiling. A ceiling below today's quote is a warning rather than a block, because only the quote at each fill counts, but an opt-in that cannot complete at all is never signed. Changing any of the three choices clears the maker's confirmation. Unticked, the order signs the fields off, as before. The fork-tier test harness's order book had stayed on the old signed shape, so it would have refused every gasless post the app now signs; it moved to the new shape too. A new fork test proves the maker's choice reaches the signed order, by checking the contract's own hash of the order the harness verified. Orders of the old shape are also left out of the market summaries the desk reads — a market whose only depth was such an order is not advertised — and the persisted summaries are rebuilt once after the upgrade so no stale count survives.

**Spec:** the connected-app spec's Full VPFI tariff opt-in section states how a signed-order maker chooses on the ticket. **Plan:** M2's remaining row reads built.

## Thread — the never-wired ETH·day tariff knob is removed (#1956)

The ETH·day tariff knob was an administrator setting that charged a quantity of VPFI per unit of ETH loan volume per day. It was never wired into absorption. The absorption formula uses the LIF·year coefficient instead, and the formula revision forbade wiring the ETH·day knob. The programme plan said to delete it once nothing called it, and nothing does.

What changed:
- Its setter, its event and its error are removed.
- The recycling configuration read now returns only the platform margin.
- Its storage slot stays, marked retired, so the layout is unchanged.
- The deploy check that used to alarm if the dead knob moved now confirms it is not routed at all.
- An in-place refresh removes the retired route automatically.
- The administrator knob reference no longer lists it.

## Thread — the reward claim window's two user safeguards: a free pre-expiry notice and a Claim Center countdown (#1956)

**Problem.** Once governance sets the reward claim window, an interaction reward that stays claimable but unclaimed for the full window plus a ninety-day notice period returns to the rewards programme. The ratified design lets that happen only after two safeguards are live: a free pre-expiry notice in the in-app notification center, and a countdown in the Claim Center. A paid channel alone would skip exactly the dormant claimants the window affects. The contract already announced each reward's last call and its removal point, and exposed each reward's earliest possible removal date. Nothing in the app or the indexer used any of it, so the window could not be switched on as designed.

**What changed.**
- **The notices.** The second notice is filed when a reward's return to the programme BEGINS (the first chunk settled; a long reward's later chunks may still be deferred), and its wording says so — the right to claim is gone, the return has begun. The notices and the countdown depend on the platform keeper's horizon heartbeat, which is tracked separately (#2517) and is a precondition of arming the claim window. The indexer now writes two inbox entries addressed to the reward's owner, the wallet the contract's event names, rather than to a loan party. One is a last-call entry when a reward enters its final notice period. The other is an outcome entry when an unclaimed reward reaches its removal point and returns to the programme.
- **A re-armed reward notifies again.** A change to the claim window restarts a reward's notice, so the last call is filed once per announcement, not once per reward.
- **The notices are independent of the loan entries.** They need no lookup, so a failed lookup for a loan entry in the same scan cannot drop them.
- **A failed write cannot drop them either (#2489 review).** The indexer keeps a mark of the block its inbox rows are complete through. Before, a failed write of a scan's rows was swallowed and that mark advanced anyway, so a one-shot notice whose event had already been scanned could be lost for good. Now a failed write holds the mark, and on the next run the missing rows are re-derived from the archived events and written before the mark moves on. A long gap is drained a bounded window at a time. A chain that has never been marked starts its mark at the present without replaying history, so a restored environment does not re-mint every old notice. A failure during a long first catch-up — several passes before the indexer reaches the chain head — is remembered as well: the mark is set just below the failed pass at once, so the pass that reaches the head re-derives from there rather than certifying the lost rows.
- **In the bell.** The entries name the reward rather than a loan, and open the Claim Center.
- **The countdown.** The Claim Center's rewards card now shows the soonest date by which one of the wallet's rewards must be claimed. The date is the contract's own earliest estimate under the current claim window, which assumes the reward stays claimable from now on: time the reward cannot be claimed moves it later, and a change to the claim window itself can replace it with an earlier date — the card says both. Inside the final notice period the card switches to a final-notice warning. The contract itself answers the soonest date over bounded pages of the wallet's open rewards, all as of one moment; a wallet's list of rewards holds only the ones not yet settled — a settled reward leaves the list, retired by the wallet's own claims and by anyone, and stays readable on its own — so the number of reads follows the wallet's live rewards rather than its lifetime, and every live reward is still considered; when the reading fails it says the check could not be completed, rather than showing nothing — which would read as "no deadline".
- **When nothing shows.** The card shows nothing while the claim window is off. If it cannot read every reward's date, it shows none rather than one that might be later than the real date.

**How it is verified.** Indexer tests run the planner and the inbox write against the real schema, and two deliberate breakages were confirmed to fail them. A fork spec opens the reward entry in the bell and follows it to the Claim Center. Unit tests pin the countdown's choice of date and its switch to final-notice wording.

**Deferred: the countdown's live check.** The countdown itself cannot be exercised on the fork, which has no reward a sweep has started counting, nor on the deployed testnet, where the window stays off until the activation ceremony. The plan already makes verifying both safeguards live a step of that ceremony.

## Thread — a mirror's keeper earmark now resolves on every day, demand or not, and what Base attributes to a mirror per day is capped (#2047)

**Problem.** Two limits of the Base-authorized keeper allocation, surfaced in review and ratified for fixing on 2026-09-07. First, the earmark only ran on days that had claim demand: the day's funding resolution returned early when nothing was owed to claimants, and the earmark sat downstream of that return, so a chain that absorbed recycled receipts on a quiet day earmarked nothing — exactly the low-demand, inflow-rich chains the channel exists to serve. Second, the earmark's numerator was the credit Base attributed to the mirror for the day, and the mirror supplied both terms of that attribution, so a mirror holding accumulated unattributed headroom could label all of it as one day's credit and accelerate its own earmark. Neither was a solvency defect — the availability clamp bounded the total in both — but both weakened what the card promised.

**The earmark's own step.** The keeper earmark is now resolved in a step of its own inside the same day finalization, after the funding stamps and on every armed day: for each mirror with an armed allocation, Base sizes the earmark from the inflow it attributed to the mirror for the day, bounds it to what the mirror's availability leaves after the day's claim commit and every earlier draw, charges it to the mirror's own draw slot, and writes it into the day's funding record — the same record the same broadcast already carries. A quiet day's record is stamped for this. One implementation serves demand days and quiet days alike; the demand gate no longer decides whether inflow is earmarked.

**One Base-set ceiling.** Governance may set, per mirror, a ceiling on the recycled credit Base attributes to that chain per day. The ceiling bounds each day's attribution; what it trims from a day's figure is carried by Base as a backlog and dripped on later days at no more than the ceiling, so everything an honest chain absorbed still attributes, over the following days, while the pace — and so the earmark's timing — is bounded from Base's side, and the mirror supplies neither the backlog's size nor its pace. A ceiling far above a chain's daily figures changes nothing. A chain's keeper allocation cannot be armed until its ceiling is set, and the ceiling cannot return to zero while the allocation is armed: the earmark is never armed uncapped. Without a ceiling, the previous attribution rule stands unchanged. The backlog is limited to the chain's headroom before it is added to a day's claim, so a mirror reporting an enormous cumulative cannot make the sum overflow and block that chain's every later day report. The knobs document gives two ordering steps for an upgraded deployment: set a ceiling for any chain whose allocation was armed before ceilings existed, and let one day finalize between setting a ceiling and arming an allocation.

The loop-closure figures (how much of what reward claims distributed stayed inside the platform) had never had a display home at all. Separately, the old dashboard decided "is the pool backed?" itself by comparing the platform's balance with the recycle bucket alone, which was wrong in both custody states: before reward custody is activated it ignored the two recovery reservations held inside the same balance, so a balance that had spent reserved value could still read as backed; after activation the bucket and the recovery position are held by the custody holder, so the same comparison would call a healthy chain short. The stored reading also carried no record of which state the chain was in.

**What changed — the indexer.** The scheduled capture now reads the versioned backing view, which adds the activation state and the holder's balance and attributed total to the eight figures already stored, all at the same settled block. Before activation the platform's balance must cover the bucket plus both reservations; after it, the holder must cover everything attributed to it and the platform must still cover its one remaining reservation. The comparison is exact, with no tolerance, so a published answer always agrees with the published figures. ## Thread — liquidation-fallback collateral waits in the borrower's vault under a lock, not in the Diamond (#1566, #2141)

**Problem.** When every swap route fails during a health-factor liquidation or a time-based default, the loan becomes fallback-pending and waits for the lender's claim, a cure, an internal match or the keeper backstop. Until now the whole collateral waited in the Diamond's own balance for that time, mixed with everything else the Diamond holds. That broke the owner's custody rule — a user's asset may pass through the Diamond inside one transaction, but must not rest there between transactions (Option F, ratified 2026-08-31) — and it was one of the two kinds of user value #2141's attribution suite recorded as resting in the Diamond.

**Entry.** The failed liquidation now puts the collateral back into the borrower's own vault in the same transaction, re-locks it for the fallback, records the amount on a new per-loan fallback custody record, and only then moves the loan to fallback-pending. It also checks that the lender, treasury and borrower shares of the fallback split add up to exactly the collateral it holds, and refuses otherwise. The Diamond keeps nothing. The health-factor liquidation and the time-based default share this one entry.

**One record, one event.** Every move of that collateral goes through the one record and emits one event naming the loan, the kind of move, the counterparty, the asset, the amount and what the record still holds. The kinds are: entry; out to the treasury, the lender or the backstop; kept for the borrower; pulled for a retry, and restored after a failed one; out to the receiving lender and to the matcher in an internal match; and returned to the ordinary collateral lock. Each move also re-derives the borrower's fee-tier exclusion through the same rule every per-loan lock already uses, so no path can forget it.

**Every way out reads the vault.**

- In-kind claim: the treasury's and lender's shares move straight out of the borrower's vault, through the forced move-out path, so a borrower sanctioned after the fallback cannot block the lender. The borrower's share stays in the vault, still locked, as the borrower's claim.
- Retry: the collateral leaves the vault only inside the retry, and the amount the Diamond receives is checked exactly. If the retry fails, the same transaction puts it back and re-locks it.
- Keeper backstop: the lender's share moves from the borrower's vault to the backstop's vault, and the amount warehoused is the amount that moved.
- Cure by top-up and full repayment: no tokens move. The lock already covers the fallback amount, which returns to the ordinary collateral lock.
- Internal match: each leg draws on the paying loan's own record and lock, with the receiving lender's part and the matcher's incentive as separate moves, through one vault path; the branch that paid legs out of Diamond custody is gone. A partial match rescales the fallback split so it adds up exactly to what remains in the vault, the borrower's share taking the rounding remainder, so every unit stays attributed and #2141's suite now allows no rounding remainder at all.

**Fee tier.** While a loan is fallback-pending with VPFI collateral, that collateral does not count toward the borrower's fee tier, because the fallback split has already allotted shares of it to others. The exclusion equals what the record holds, follows every partial move, and is installed without the tier ever dipping below what the borrower owns. Two readings now compare the lock with the record: whether a fallback-pending loan was topped up (otherwise every fallback loan would read as topped up and the keeper backstop would refuse them all), and how much of it an internal match may use.

**A time-based default now refreshes the borrower's fee tier.** A time-based default that moved VPFI collateral out of the borrower's vault, by swap or in kind, did not refresh the borrower's fee tier, so the borrower kept tier and staking credit on VPFI already gone until some unrelated later action. One refresh now follows both routes. It refreshes locally and leaves the cross-chain push to the borrower's next action that pushes, so a permissionless default can never fail on the push budget. The health-factor liquidation's refresh still pushes in the same transaction; that difference is recorded as an open item for the owner.

**How this reaches a chain.** The owner decided on 2026-10-07 that every chain running this change gets a fresh deploy. Nothing is migrated: a fallback row recorded under the old custody is refused, by name, at every way out, rather than paid from the Diamond. The fallback custody facet and the health-factor liquidation, default, claim, collateral top-up, repayment and internal-match facets must be refreshed together on any test or rehearsal Diamond, and the narrower curated refresh refuses any Diamond from before this change outright: it carries neither the collateral top-up nor the repayment facet, which would otherwise keep paying a cure out of the Diamond's own balance. Storage gains one appended per-loan field.

**Limits, stated.** A token issuer that blacklists the borrower's vault strands the lender's and treasury's shares in it until the blacklist lifts. A borrower vault behind a mandatory upgrade refuses moves out until it is upgraded; anyone may run that upgrade, so the lender can be delayed but not blocked. Mirror chains see the borrower's new tier only at the borrower's next pushing action. The reward-retention metric reads low for a VPFI-collateral borrower whose collateral makes a fallback round trip (at the failed liquidation, and at a failed retry), because the outflow is recorded and the return is not; it stays a conservative lower bound, and no funds are affected. The indexer records each custody move on the loan's activity timeline, under the action that made it, and does not otherwise act on it. Refs #1566, #2141.

## Thread — a swap-to-repay auction's collateral stays in the borrower's vault until its own fill (#1566, #2141)

**Problem.** When a borrower put collateral up for a resolver-filled swap-to-repay auction, the commit moved the auction's lot out of the borrower's vault into the Diamond, where it waited until the auction filled or was cancelled. That broke the owner's custody rule — a user's asset may pass through the Diamond inside one transaction, but must not rest there between transactions — and it was the second kind of user value #2141's attribution suite recorded as resting in the Diamond. It also meant a cancellation had to put the lot back, and a borrower flagged after committing made that return fail, which could block the lender's protection (#658).

**The commit moves nothing.** Committing an auction now records the order and nothing else: the lot stays in the borrower's vault, pledged with the rest of the collateral, and the borrower's fee tier is unchanged. The order still sells exactly the debt-sized lot. Because the commit no longer withdraws from the vault, it no longer screens the borrower as a side effect of that withdrawal, and the signature and approval it now sets up on the vault (below) are not screened either: a borrower already flagged can now commit. That is deliberate — the full swap-to-repay is a must-complete close-out that the screen never blocks, and the fill delivers the proceeds through the same freeze-at-destination rules as before.

**The fill takes the lot, and nothing else can happen meanwhile.** The lot leaves the vault only inside the fill that sells it, and goes from the vault straight to the resolver, never through the protocol's own balance; a borrower flagged after committing cannot block the fill that repays their lender. The protocol checks the vault gave up exactly the lot (a collateral token that takes more or less than that fails the fill, and the auction can still be cancelled; a token that charges its fee to the receiver costs only the resolver who chose to fill), records the move with a new event naming the vault and the resolver, and refreshes a VPFI holder's tier at the reduced balance. From the start of the fill until the fill's settlement step begins, the protocol refuses every other call into its functions — from a token's transfer callback, from the resolver's own step, from a native-currency payout — so nothing can act on a loan halfway through being repaid; the settlement itself then runs under the protocol's ordinary protection against re-entry. The settlement then pays the debt from exactly the principal the resolver delivered; principal arriving any other way during the fill makes it fail. One trade-off is stated: a resolver cannot settle one Vaipakam auction inside another's fill. An emergency pause now stops fills; an auction stays live until its deadline.

**The borrower's vault is the order's seller.** With the protocol named as the seller of an order whose lot it no longer held, 1inch's order book would have listed every auction as one its seller could not fill, and resolvers that take only fillable orders would never have seen it. The order now names the borrower's own vault as the seller: the vault holds the lot and approves 1inch's settlement contract for exactly the lots of its own live auctions, so the order book sees an order its seller can fill. The vault signs only for its own live auctions, by asking the protocol, which itself no longer signs any swap-to-repay order; the treasury's buyback orders are unchanged. This follows the custody pattern the recycling plan settled on — a user's value waits in a dedicated container the protocol controls, not in the protocol's own balance — and the vault already plays the seller's part for a prepay listing. The service that posts an auction to 1inch's order book now accepts the borrower's vault as the seller, confirming it against the auction's on-chain record before it uses the protocol's 1inch key; when that check cannot run it declines to post rather than post an order it has not verified, and the borrower can simply retry. Its messages for a chain 1inch does not serve now say the collateral stays pledged in the borrower's vault.

**Cancelling moves nothing.** Every way an auction ends without a fill — the borrower's cancel, the permissionless cancel after the grace period, and the cancellations that liquidation and default force — now only withdraws the order — the vault stops signing for it and its approval is lowered by the lot — and there is no lot to return. So a borrower flagged after committing no longer blocks any of them, which closes #658. The check that an order was not already filled now reads the limit-order protocol's record of the order's nonce, which is what that protocol actually writes for these orders; the check it replaces could never see a fill.

**How this reaches a chain.** As for slice 2, every chain that runs this change gets a fresh deploy (owner decision, 2026-10-07). The refusal of other calls during a fill sits in the Diamond's own entry point, which is why it needs a new Diamond. The intent facet, the facet that receives the limit-order protocol's callbacks and the vault-factory facet must be refreshed together on any test or rehearsal Diamond, and the vault implementation gains the approval the commit sets up, which a fresh deploy's vaults have from the start. Nothing is added to storage; a new read reports how much of an auction rests in the Diamond — always nothing on a fresh deploy — for the custody census.

**Limits, stated.** Mirror chains see the borrower's reduced tier only at the borrower's next pushing action, as for the fallback moves. A commit can now be accepted that no fill can complete — a mandatory vault upgrade imposed during the auction (anyone may run the upgrade, which unblocks it), or a collateral or principal token that does not move exact amounts — because the commit no longer moves anything; nothing is lost, the auction stays cancellable, and the intent token allowlists are meant to admit only exact-transfer tokens. The lot's departure is shown on the loan's activity timeline under the fill; the fill event, which the indexer already handles, still closes the loan. A test or rehearsal Diamond cannot be brought across this change by an in-place refresh at all: the refusal of other calls lives in the Diamond contract itself, so both refresh scripts now refuse a Diamond from before it. ## Thread — the Diamond's balance must be explained, and the liquidation fallback gets one home (#2141, #1566)

**Problem.** The rule behind #2141 is that, for every asset, the Diamond's balance should be fully explained by what the protocol itself owns. A user's asset may pass through the Diamond inside one transaction, but it must not rest there between transactions. Two kinds of user value rested there when this work began: the whole collateral of a liquidation or default whose swaps all failed, until a claim, a cure, an internal match or the backstop resolved the loan; and a swap-to-repay intent's lot, from its commit until the order filled or was cancelled. These include liquidations and defaults whose swaps all fail, claim retries, in-kind claims, cures, backstop absorbs and internal matches. It runs with an external treasury, the mainnet arrangement. For each tracked asset (a stablecoin, wrapped ether and VPFI) it asserts four things. On the code this suite was first written against, a partial internal match rounded the three fallback slices down separately, so up to two wei per partial leg stayed in the Diamond with no owner; the first check allowed exactly that much and no more. With the vault-held custody in this release the borrower slice absorbs the rounding remainder, and that allowance is gone.

- The Diamond's balance equals the protocol's own ledgers plus the user custody still known to rest there. For VPFI, the ledgers are the recycle bucket, the stranded-token recovery reserve, and the grandfathered borrower-fee custody and rebates. The other two assets have no protocol ledger in this harness.
- The balances the harness never funds stay at zero. These are the buyback and reward budgets, the keeper reward budget, the treasury's own balance and payroll. So a new owner showing up there forces the harness to account for it, rather than hiding inside the total.
- Every vault's tracked balance still covers what is encumbered in it.
- Every action family ran at least once, so a green run cannot be a run that never reached the fallback.

The suite runs with the other invariant suites in the pre-deploy invariant pass. The per-PR CI lane does not compile the invariant suites.

**The strict form is now active.** The end state #2141 asks for is a user-custody term of zero. That form was written first against the old custody and recorded failing there, which proved it can fail. It is switched on in this release: for the liquidation fallback because that collateral now waits in the borrower's vault under a lien, and for intents because the fill pulls the lot from the vault at the moment of use. A deterministic cell pins that every custody class is vault-held and that a legacy intent row still in the Diamond turns the check red. Intent commits, fills and cancels run against a mock of the limit-order contract, with fill cells and a fork cell against the deployed router.

**The fallback flows move to one host, with no behaviour change.** The liquidation fallback used to be written out twice: once in the health-factor liquidation and once in the time-based default. Both of those facets are close to the contract size limit. The claim path also carried its own retry swap and its own in-kind split. A new internal facet, FallbackCustodyFacet, now hosts all of these:

- the fallback entry, which both liquidation paths call when every swap route fails;
- the claim's retry swap;
- the in-kind split of the fallback collateral;
- a read view that reports how much of a loan's fallback collateral is vault-held and how much still rests in the Diamond.

Only the Diamond itself can call the three entries that change state. The claim keeps the distribution of a successful retry's proceeds, the retry-attempted mark and the retry event. Every storage write, event and token movement is the same as before, in the same order, and so is every revert that carries a reason; a revert with no reason at all now reaches the caller as an empty four-byte error code instead of no data. The three fallback events keep their exact signatures, so anything listening for them keeps matching. At this step the collateral still rested in the Diamond, so the view reported all of it as Diamond-held; slice 2 of the same release (see the fallback-custody-in-vault note) moves it into the borrower's own vault under a lock, after which the view reports it as vault-held. The split exists so that slice 2 changes one producer instead of two, without growing facets that have no room.

**Refresh together, and how this reaches a chain.** The health-factor liquidation, default and claim facets now call entries that only the new facet carries. The four must be refreshed together: a caller refreshed without the new facet reverts on every fallback entry, retry and in-kind claim. With the custody moved in this release, the collateral top-up, repayment and internal-match facets join that set, because every reader of the fallback row must use the same custody source. The owner decided on 2026-10-07 that every chain that runs slices 2 and 3 gets a fresh deploy. So no chain is upgraded in place across this change, and no pre-slice row is migrated. No storage is added. Refs #2141, #1566.

**Spec:** the invariant suite and the host split change no behaviour of their own; the rule that no user asset rests in protocol custody across transactions enters the functional spec through the two sections above, which do. ## Thread — intent and buyback orders read the limit-order protocol's identity the way the deployed protocol declares it (#2449)

**Problem.** The resolver-filled swap-to-repay intent and the treasury's validated buyback both compute the order hash the 1inch limit-order protocol will present, and sign for it. To do that they need the protocol's signing identity (its EIP-712 domain), and they asked for it through a getter that only the standalone build of the protocol has. The protocol actually deployed on mainnet is the one inside 1inch's Aggregation Router v6: it declares its identity through the standard ERC-5267 read, under the router's own name, and has no such getter. So both commits failed against the deployed protocol before signing anything, and the two features could not run on any chain configured with it. No test caught it, because every test stand-in for the protocol carried the getter.

**Fix.** One shared read now takes the identity from the protocol itself through the standard read that every build answers, accepts only the plain form (a name, a version, this chain and the protocol's own address), and derives the signing domain from it. Both commits use it. A fork test against the deployed router proves that the derived domain reproduces the router's own order hash, and the test stand-ins now declare an identity the same way instead of hard-coding the getter, so they can no longer hide this. Found by the #1566 slice-3 fork test. Closes #2449.

**Spec:** ProjectDetailsREADME — the resolver-filled intent paragraph (the protocol signs for exactly the order the configured limit-order protocol presents, the treasury's validated buyback likewise). The code-vs-docs audit records it as a resolved code defect.

## Weekly kickoff card replaces the previous one (PR #2522)

The weekly "Iteration kickoff sync" card used to be filed every Monday without ever closing the one before it, so unworked cards piled up; nineteen were open on 2026-10-16 and had to be closed by hand. Each run now closes every older open kickoff card right after filing the new one, and leaves a comment on each that links its successor. A close that fails is retried by the next run, so a missed week cannot leave a card stranded.

Nothing in the ritual is lost when a card closes unworked. The ritual walks every memory note changed since the last sync sentinel, not since the previous Monday, so the new card covers whatever the old one left. The cards now carry a dedicated `iteration-kickoff` label, recorded in the labels guide, which is how older cards are found; no other issue should carry it.
