# Release Notes — 2026-10-18

Today's changes fall into four groups. The capacity arming fee gained fixed bounds of 0.5% to 10%, and its rounding became fair across a whole deposit. The first perk with a real effect shipped: a priority credit that lets a lender's intent fill through when the solver's own capacity would refuse it. It stays unpriced until the keeper can deliver it. BNB testnet received the same fresh stack as Base and Arbitrum, as a second reward mirror, and six deploy-tooling gaps found during those redeploys were closed. The keeper gained its own private database and an always-on machine runtime, and the sanctions tooling moved forward with a mainnet list, an adder service, a delister bot and a delisting path the Safe can veto.

## Thread — the capacity arming fee gets fixed bounds of 0.5% to 10% (#1219)

The arming fee a solver, matcher or keeper pays when a capacity deposit buys more capacity is unchanged in shape. It is a one-time charge in VPFI, a percentage of the principal the post newly arms, paid beside the deposit and never out of it, and credited to the recycle bucket. What changes is how its range is held. The first build kept a mutable floor that the admin could only raise, and a ceiling of the whole armed principal. Following the owner's direction of 2026-10-18, the range is now two fixed bounds written into the contract: a positive rate must be at least 0.5% and at most 10%. The separate floor setter is gone, and the rate is the one remaining setting. Zero is still accepted, and only as the switch that keeps arming dark. The fee getter keeps its earlier shape, so readers built for either version can read either, and a new getter reports both bounds. The fee is now counted over the whole deposit rather than post by post. Each post pays what it owes less anything the deposit has already prepaid, rounded up to whole smallest units, and the overpayment is credited to the deposit's next post. So a deposit never pays less than the rate applied to everything it armed, and never more than one smallest unit above it over its life. Neither splitting a deposit into many small posts nor spreading it across fresh operator addresses arms principal for free, even in a token with coarse units. Any single post pays at most its own share rounded up, which is one smallest unit of the live VPFI token above the rate: one wei at 18 decimals, but one whole token if a rotation ever installs a token without decimals. A rotation forfeits any sub-unit credit left on the retired epoch. These bounds apply to arming under this version; no chain had armed any principal before it. The three errors the earlier fee and floor setters threw stay declared, so clients can still decode a revert from a chain whose facet has not been refreshed yet. Each deposit's prepaid credit can be read directly, so a later post that quotes less, or nothing, is explained by state anyone can see. No small remainder of principal is ever left unable to arm. The bounds are also enforced where the fee is charged: a chain upgraded in place that still holds a rate from before the change, outside the new range, treats it as no rate at all, refusing posts until an admin sets one inside the bounds, rather than quietly adjusting it.

The reason is the same guarantee with less to reason about. The fee can never be configured down to a token or up past a tenth of what it arms. Because the bounds are constants rather than admin state, nobody has to check which floor a given chain was left on. Moving them later takes a contract upgrade, which is intended for a bound with a legal purpose. A payer who quoted a fee is still protected mid-flight by the ceiling they bound at the quote. The intended starting rate is 2%. The owner approved this fee shape on 2026-10-18, which discharges the fee-shape review that had gated arming. Setting the rate on a chain, and enabling service capacity there, remain admin steps in the activation sequence. Nothing in this change sets a rate, and service capacity stays dark everywhere.

The former floor's storage slot stays in place, retired, so the storage layout remains append-only. On the testnets already carrying the earlier facet, the old floor setter stays routed until the next facet refresh sweeps it.

## Thread — the keeper indexes and proves every reward delivery's days on each mirror (#1944, transport epochs)

**Problem.** A reward-budget delivery that lands on a mirror funds the days it lists. The chain learns which days only when somebody supplies the list against the commitment the delivery stamped on arrival; nothing per day can be written at arrival, because the arrival's gas is already spent. Three permissionless steps do that work, and nothing ran them:
- indexing a delivery's epoch, page by page or chunk by chunk;
- proving a delivery that holds no epoch, by its whole list;
- proving a rooted delivery chunk by chunk.

Until an epoch's days are indexed its value funds nothing a claim can find. The mirror delivery gate also holds every funded day until its delivery is proven, so the runbook kept the gate dark: no keeper step existed.

**What changed.** The keeper has a new pass, armed by its own flag, that runs every few minutes on every mirror.
- **Finding deliveries.** It reads the chain's own record of every budget delivery from the indexer, with no age window, a page at a time (see the open-work note). Each delivery is joined to the event carrying its day list: the flat list itself, or the rooted wire's marker.
- **Flat lists.** For a delivery holding an epoch, it indexes the next page of the list until the index is whole. For one holding no epoch, it proves the whole list, unless every listed day has already landed.
- **Rooted lists.** It rebuilds the list from the canonical chain, following the runbook's rule: the reservation's day list, filtered to the days actually funded for this chain. It splits the list into chunks and builds the root with the wire's own encoding. It sends nothing unless that root matches both the reservation's and the delivery's. It then indexes, or proves, each chunk not yet landed, with that chunk's proof.

Every call is simulated first and moves no value. Each send waits to be mined before the next. The keeper's per-account send queue orders it with the keeper's other jobs, which sign from the same account, so no two sends take the same nonce. The wire encoding is checked against values computed independently, so an encoding drift fails a test rather than a proof on chain.

**Operator steps.** Arm the pass on every mirror before reward transport is live there. The delivery gate may then be armed on a mirror once the pass runs there. The restore runbook captures the new flag.

**Crowded days.** A day crowded with newer deliveries can need position hints to be indexed. When the chain refuses a call for that reason, the pass retries it once with hints it derives from the chain's own views, and logs the call if it is still refused.

**Limits, stated.**
- A reservation sent before the canonical chain recorded which days it funded is skipped, with a log line saying so. Its list can only come from the original cross-chain message, which this pass does not read, so such a day is proven by hand. The pass never falls back to the live per-day record, which a release or a later remittance rewrites.
- The pass does not yet raise alerts for a day left waiting or for a proof that is refused; both show only in the keeper's logs (#2516).

Refs #1944, #1566, #1349.

## Sanctions adder — a refused download leaves nothing behind

The adder's download tool writes the OFAC publication to a partial file and
renames it only once the whole body has arrived; a download it refuses (the
server answers with an error, the body exceeds the size cap, the file cannot
be written) removes the partial file before reporting the refusal. The
removal ran before the file had necessarily been created: the write stream
opens its file in the background, so a refusal that arrived first saw the
partial file appear after it had been removed. The tool now waits for the
stream to close before removing, so a refused download leaves nothing on
disk. This showed up as an intermittent failure of the tool's own test in
CI, on a change that did not touch it.

## Thread — the mainnet sanctions list: Vaipakam's own OFAC record, plus RedStone where deployed (PR #2543)

The owner decided the mainnet sanctions source on 2026-10-17 (#2443): Vaipakam keeps its own on-chain list of the digital-currency addresses on OFAC's SDN list, and combines it with RedStone's Sanctions Oracle on chains where RedStone has deployed one. A wallet is flagged if either flags it. Chainalysis's on-chain oracle, which this replaces, was retired in March 2026 and is not configured anywhere. This change adds the list contract and the offline tooling that computes its updates. It does not deploy anything or configure any chain.

The new list contract is meant to be the oracle a mainnet Diamond screens against. Its owner is the chain's admin, with two-step transfer and renouncing disabled. The owner appoints two kinds of updater and does not update the list itself unless it appoints itself. An adder, meant to be the automated updater, may only add addresses or record that nothing changed. A remover, meant to be the multisig, may also remove addresses. Removing an address lets a sanctioned wallet back in, so it takes the multisig's signature (owner decision 2026-10-18). When one OFAC publication both adds and removes, the adder applies the additions and the multisig then applies the removals from the same publication, and the diff tool prepares the transactions separately. A publication too large for one transaction, such as the first population, is added in batches. Until a publication is fully applied, the list states publicly how many of its additions and removals are still waiting, so it never presents a partly applied publication as complete. Each update records the SHA-256 of the exact OFAC file it was built from, that file's publication date, and the time it was applied, so anyone can read on-chain how current the list is and what it was built from. An update that changes nothing is still recorded. Every address added or removed emits its own event, and the current list can be read in pages from the contract. An update names the list version it was computed against and is refused against any other, all or nothing. Adding a listed address, removing an unlisted one, or adding and removing the same address in one update is refused, so a stale or doubled update fails loudly. Publications only move forward. The publication date is stored in a form that can be ordered, and an update from an older publication is refused, because it would re-list delisted addresses and drop newer designations while still fitting the current list. The same publication is accepted again only to finish what its first update declared outstanding (a refresh that changes nothing, the adder's next batch of additions, or the multisig completing its removals), and never to add or remove more than was declared; a different file with the recorded date is refused. Publication dates must be real calendar dates, so an impossible date such as 31 February is refused. A deliberate rollback or a same-day OFAC reissue needs a one-time authorization from the owner naming that exact file and the list version the owner reviewed; if the list has moved on by the time the authorization lands, it is refused. The owner cannot authorize a date newer than the recorded one, since a newer publication needs no permission. Any successful update withdraws an authorization that is still outstanding and logs that it did, so an unused permission cannot be spent later. Both the authorization and its use are logged. RedStone's oracle, where used, is fixed at deployment and given as an explicit input. No address is built in, because the only deployment we know of (Ethereum mainnet) has not been verified by us. The contract reports RedStone's own freshness when RedStone provides it, and reports "unknown" when it does not. It can say which of the two sources flags an address. When RedStone cannot be read, an address the own list flags is still flagged, and for any other address the failure goes to the Diamond unchanged. That keeps the Diamond's existing fail-open and fail-closed screens exactly where they were. Answering from the own list alone would quietly turn the fail-closed ones fail-open during a RedStone outage.

The testnet sanctions test list from #2439 now shares its combining rule and its failure behaviour with the mainnet list, through one common base. Its addresses, functions, events and errors are unchanged, and the overlay's existing tests pass unchanged. A new offline tool reads OFAC's SDN Advanced XML (a local copy, or downloaded) and extracts every EVM-format address under any digital-currency label. It drops entries OFAC marks as false and reports malformed values for a person to check. It reads the current list from the contract on the chain the operator names (and refuses an RPC serving any other chain), always at the finalized block, fixed by that block's hash so every read describes the same state. It emits a removal only for an address the OFAC file no longer mentions and that the operator has confirmed against OFAC's Recent Actions notice for that publication; any other removal is refused, and reliability values outside OFAC's published vocabulary refuse the file. It does not accept a list of current addresses from a file, because nothing would tie that file to the list version it claims. It prints what to add and remove, the file's fingerprint and date, and the ready update calls: the additions in adder batches, then the removals in multisig batches, each sized to fit a transaction. It reads a digital-currency value as an address only when OFAC files it as free text, and refuses a record whose detail type is anything else or missing. It refuses a file in any OFAC schema version other than the one whose layout it reads, and a file whose bytes are not valid text, so a corrupted address is never silently dropped. It refuses an incomplete file: the whole input is checked for complete XML structure before anything is extracted, so a truncated download fails wherever it was cut. One reader both checks that structure and extracts the data, so a feature inside a comment or inside a CDATA section is never read as a listing, and escaped characters are decoded exactly or the file is refused. It refuses a file it cannot find addresses in, and a publication older than the one the list records. It refuses a diff removing more than ten addresses unless that bound is deliberately raised. Every numeric option must be a plain non-negative whole number, so a typo cannot switch off a guard. Every value that starts with 0x but is not exactly an address is reported as written, for a person to check. It can also compare once against the retired Chainalysis oracle's logged history, or an exported copy of it, and print three sets: in both, OFAC only, and Chainalysis only. The Chainalysis-only addresses are for review and are never added, because that list stopped recording delistings when it froze. The configure script still refuses every mainnet, now saying why: the signers are decided (the automated adder lists, the multisig alone removes), but where the adder runs is still an open owner question. Showing the list's freshness in the flagged-wallet banner, and naming OFAC or RedStone there, is a follow-up. Today the banner names "the list the deployment screens against". Refs #2443.

## Thread — Sanctions adder service, testnet first (PR #2557)

The platform's own sanctions list now has the service that keeps it current. Once a day it downloads and parses OFAC's publication once, keeps the exact file, and updates every configured chain from it, so all chains follow the same publication and one chain's failure never stops another. It is configured like the keeper: one settings file holding the adder key and one RPC per chain, created on the machine by the installer and filled in there. The list it writes to on each chain comes from the committed deployment artifacts rather than a typed address. On each chain it applies every new addition with the list's adder key, and for a new publication records it on-chain with its delistings declared outstanding for the Safe. It never delists, never rolls back, and refuses every mainnet: the mainnet host is still to be decided. Any refusal by the update tool (a damaged or moved record, an unsupported delisting, an older publication), a list that moved past the finalized block, or a key that is not the adder alone (also a remover or the owner) stops that chain before anything is sent. A run counts as applied only once its transactions are final; one that stops part-way says which transactions it already broadcast and whether each is final, included or may still land. Each run reports to the journal and, when configured, the ops Telegram chat (a failed alert is recorded and fails the run), and anything the list still declares outstanding, delistings or additions, is reported on every run until it is resolved.

The OFAC parser now reads the publication in pieces instead of holding it in memory. On the 128 MB October 2026 file, peak memory fell from about 500 MB to about 150 MB, with identical results, so the service can share the keeper's 1 GB machine. The service runs as its own systemd units with its own key and dynamic user, a memory cap, and lower CPU and IO weight than the keeper, so it can never take the keeper down. It is deployed by an operator naming a reviewed commit on main, never automatically on merge. A new testnet-only deploy script puts a SanctionsList beside the Diamond's test list as the service's rehearsal target. It appoints the adder and the remover, keeps them distinct from each other and the adder distinct from the owner, and does not change what the Diamond screens against.

## Thread — Sanctions delister bot, testnet first (PR #2567)

The delisting side of the sanctions list now has its unattended service. Every hour it reads each configured chain's delisting timelock and moves every pending proposal on as far as it can: it clears away proposals whose bot key the owner removed, withdraws its own proposals that can no longer complete (an address the Safe vetoed, one RedStone flags, one already delisted another way, or one OFAC's newer file no longer delists), moves its own proposals onto a newer publication once it has re-checked every address in them, and after the waiting window requests RedStone's answer over Chainlink CCIP where RedStone is on another chain (only once it can reach that chain to relay the answer, since each request costs a fee), relays the answer back, and completes the proposal once the timelock reports it ready. Every step that moves a proposal toward a delisting (requesting an answer, relaying it, completing the proposal) is taken, whoever made the proposal, only after a fresh reading of OFAC's files in that same state supports delisting every address the proposal names. A failed, older or newer download, two files that disagree, a same-day reissue the adder will not apply by itself, or a list part-way through an update, and none of those steps is taken; nor is any while the timelock is not a remover of the list, so revoking that role stops paid requests and relays as well as new proposals. Another proposer's proposal naming an address the files do not support is never moved on, and is reported for the Safe to veto. It then proposes the remaining delistings, and only those that both of OFAC's exports support: the advanced file the list records must no longer contain the address, and the plain SDN.XML, read by its own parser, must not mention it anywhere. The two files must be the same publication and list the same addresses, neither may carry a digital-currency value it cannot read as an address (one may be a damaged copy of an address being delisted, so while one is there every delisting waits for a person), the number of delistings must match what the list itself declares outstanding, and addresses already proposed, vetoed by the Safe, or flagged by RedStone today are left out. Anything only one file supports, or that RedStone flags, is named in the report and left for the Safe. With no RedStone check configured on a chain, nothing can complete there, so it proposes nothing and says the delistings await the Safe.

OFAC's files are downloaded only when a chain has publication work, and a chain whose state has not changed is re-checked once a day, so a delisting the Safe vetoed does not cost a 150 MB download every hour. Only a conclusive check of a chain the run left untouched is remembered: a failed download, two files caught either side of a new publication, or any transaction sent (included is not yet final) means the next hour checks again. The key must be the timelock's proposer and nothing else: not the timelock's owner, and not the adder, remover or owner of the list on any chain it has an RPC for, the chain it relays RedStone's answers from included, checked before the key is used there at all. The timelock must serve the list the release records; otherwise that chain is refused. Every transaction is simulated before it is signed, recorded by hash before it is broadcast, and reported as included, reverted, refused by the node (only when the node names why and answers that it does not hold it), failed or of unknown outcome; one failed step never stops the others (a candidate check that cannot be read proposes nothing and leaves every other step to run), except that after a transaction of unknown outcome it sends nothing more on that chain that run, so a later one cannot take its nonce; anything that did not land is retried next run. A run in which a failure put off work a chain needed, such as no conclusive reading of OFAC's files, is reported as not done and fails the service, so it shows among failed units; waiting for a window, for RedStone or for the adder does not. The RPC of every chain it touches, the attestor's included, must serve that chain. Reports go to the journal every run and to the ops chat when something was sent, failed or needs a person, a refusal before any chain was read included, repeated once a day while a condition lasts; a chat message too long for Telegram is cut, and the journal keeps it whole. Its state files are caches only: deleting them costs a download, never a wrong action. A state file that cannot be written, or an unexpected error on one chain, is reported and fails the service, but never stops the report of what the run sent; the adder records a failed write of its run record the same way.

The adder and the delister now install together from one release of a named commit, with one installer, so they always read a publication with the same parser. Each keeps its own key, settings file, dynamic user, memory cap and units; the kit moved from ops/sanctions-adder to ops/sanctions, and a machine that ran the adder-only installer is moved over by the new one. What both services share (the chain table, the key check, how a file is kept, how a transaction is sent, the ops alert and secret redaction) now lives in one module, and both now say when a node refused a signed transaction, or when one was included and reverted, instead of calling it possibly broadcast. Both OFAC parsers' delisting witness now counts an address split across pieces of a value's text, such as a CDATA section or a comment inside it, as still mentioned. The delister's settings template uses exactly the names of the file already created on the keeper's machine.

Refs #2443. Still to come: the CCIP clearance wiring (an attestor where RedStone runs, the channel wiring on both messengers, and the timelock's clearance source).

## Thread — Automated delisting the Safe can veto (PR #2562)

Delisting a wallet from the platform's own sanctions list can now be proposed by a bot instead of being prepared by hand, without taking the decision away from the Safe. A new delisting timelock is appointed as a second remover of the list. Its bot may propose only wallets the list records, against the OFAC publication the list records, and all its pending proposals together may name no more wallets than that publication declared outstanding. Nothing happens for a waiting window (48 hours by default). During the window the Safe or the admin can cancel, and that cancel is a lasting veto on each wallet: the bot cannot bring a vetoed wallet back until the Safe lifts it. Removing the bot's key from the timelock voids everything it proposed, and anyone can clear those proposals away. A publication's delistings wait until its additions are on the list. When the window ends, RedStone's Sanctions Oracle must also not flag the wallet. Where RedStone runs on the same chain it is read directly. Elsewhere the timelock sends a request over Chainlink CCIP, once the window has ended, to a small companion contract on a chain where RedStone runs; that contract reads RedStone when the request arrives and anyone can pay to send its answer back, without being able to change it. Because the request leaves only after the window, the reading is known to post-date it without comparing two chains' clocks or relying on any value someone could guess; any answer that flags a wallet blocks that request for good. Either way, RedStone's own last-update time must be known and within a week (owner-adjustable), so a frozen oracle cannot wave a delisting through. With no RedStone source configured, automated delisting stays closed and the Safe's own direct delist, which is unchanged, remains the only route. One reading on the timelock reports which of these routes applies, and the deploy script prints it. A new testnet deploy script installs the timelock for the recorded rehearsal list; it configures no RedStone source, so on testnets proposals and vetoes can be rehearsed while delistings themselves stay with the Safe.

## Thread — the keeper gets its own private database for its operational state (#2450)

**What changed.** The keeper now keeps its own operational state in a second Cloudflare D1 database that only it can reach, alongside the shared database it already used. The private database holds eight tables: the health-factor band each at-risk loan was last seen in, the commitment-report scan positions and finished-day markers, the remittance-acknowledgement scan position and attempt log, the record of which Diamond each chain's state belongs to, and the one-time copy's completion record with the per-row fingerprints it took. Its schema now lives with the keeper.

**What stayed shared, and why.** A table moves only if nothing outside the keeper reads it. The in-app inbox, user alert thresholds and their notification records, Telegram links, the oracle snapshot and liquidity-confidence records, the reconciled-day records the indexer writes, and everything the indexer owns all stay in the shared database. The inbox rows the keeper files for a health-factor band crossing still go to the shared inbox; only the band memory behind them moved.

**Why a separate database.** Database access is granted per database, not per table. On the shared database the indexer and the agent could write any keeper record, and some of those records tell the keeper that work is finished and can be skipped. In the private database, nothing but the keeper can write them.

**How it is kept honest.** The keeper's code cannot send a private-table query to the shared database, because the two connections are distinguished at compile time. The one statement that used to read both kinds of table at once now reads each database separately and behaves exactly as before. The keeper's tests build the shared database without the moved tables, so a query sent to the wrong place fails rather than quietly passing. A test refuses any future shared migration that recreates or alters a moved table; dropping the old copies is the only change it allows. The repository checks recognise the private database: only the keeper may bind it, only under its own name and identity, and never as a second connection to the shared data. A maintenance build drops both connections, and the keeper skips its whole run if either is missing.

**Moving the live rows, once.** Five of the tables already hold live rows in the shared database. A keeper starting from empty copies would re-send acknowledgements it has already paid for and re-alert borrowers it has already warned, so the rows are copied once, with the keeper stopped. The copy command copies all five tables. It checks that each matches on both sides by row count, latest write time and a fingerprint of every row, and only then records that the copy is complete. It refuses to run over a copy it has already recorded. The keeper's deploy applies the private schema itself, because the keeper is that database's only writer. It also refuses to publish while the shared copies hold rows and no matching record exists. After the copy, rows that have only gone are accepted, since the indexer's own reset after a redeploy deletes a chain's rows. A row that is new or changed, which only a keeper writing the old copy could cause, still stops it. The old shared copies are dropped later, in a release of their own (#2560). The deployment and restore runbooks carry the steps.

## Thread — the keeper keeps every held reward claimant observed, and settles the hold once its verdict is final (#2450)

**Problem.** The reward sanctions rule acts on a flagged owner's interaction reward only once the flag is confirmed (then the days earned while flagged are forfeited and the rest is set apart for the owner): a chain of authoritative flagged readings spanning three days, with no gap between readings longer than one day. Until then a first-flagged claimant's staged payout is held, and its staging record keeps the staging encumbrance raised. That encumbrance blocks custody activation and every reward-role change. Nothing observed held owners by itself, so a hold lasted three days only if someone happened to observe the owner at least daily. A longer gap restarted the window, and the block went on with it.

**What changed.** The keeper has a new pass, armed by its own flag, that runs hourly on every reward chain.
- **Who it watches.** It reads the chain's own events from the indexer, and decides each wallet and each record on its own. It watches every wallet whose observation chain is still open, and every owner of a staging record or domain that has opened and not yet closed. The second set covers a wallet whose chain has cleared but whose record is still held from before, and a claimant held with no chain at all. A wallet flagged while it holds only ordinary rewards leaves neither trace, because its refused claim leaves nothing behind. So the pass also screens every lender and borrower against the sanctions classifier, a slice each run, sized so that every party is checked at least once a day. A party that reads as held joins the watch list, and its first observation starts the confirmation window. Nothing is dropped for its age or because newer events crowd it out. When a wallet leaves the watch list, any operator alert still open for it is cleared, so a later incident for that wallet alerts straight away. An earlier version took only the newest five thousand events, the staging records opened in the last thirty days, and the wallets cleared in the last thirty days, so a long hold could drop out of the pass.
- **How it decides.** It classifies each wallet through the contract's one sanctions classifier, the same read the sweep and the claim use.
- **Held wallets.** It records a fresh observation once the latest is six hours old. That leaves three more chances before the one-day gap could break the chain.
- **Confirmed wallets.** It runs the owner settlement, repeated while it makes progress: the days the wallet earned while flagged are forfeited, and the rest is set apart for it. It then resolves the claimant's held staging records and domains, whose last page applies the same rule to a staged day and closes the record.
- **Cleared wallets.** It resolves the same records, which now pay the claimant.
- **Cost.** Every write is simulated first and sent only if it would change something, so a settled owner costs reads, not gas. Each send waits to be mined before the next, so the pass never races the keeper's other jobs, which sign from the same account. A send that does not land in time ends the pass's sends for that tick. Its alerts write to the database only when one is raised or an open one clears. Every call is permissionless and moves value only into protocol custody or to the claimant's own venue, never to the keeper.

**Operator alerts.** The pass alerts the operator chat in three cases:
- a held claimant's next observation is due within one keeper interval, meaning the keeper's own observations are not landing;
- a hold outlasts its confirmation by more than the maximum gap, meaning an outage or a wedged record is holding the custody block;
- a wallet is held with no observation chain at all, meaning the sanctions oracle is not answering.

Alerts go through the ops bot, never the user-facing one, and each condition repeats at most every six hours. A new keeper table, in the keeper's private database, records which alerts were sent; it is disposable state.

**Operator steps.** Apply the keeper's own database migration to its private database (see the keeper private database note), then arm the pass on every reward chain before reward staging is live there. Set the ops bot token and chat id on the keeper with secret put; without them, alerts reach only the Worker log, and each attempt says so. The restore runbook now captures the new flag and the ops channel.

Closes #2450. Refs #1566, #1349.

**How the pass works now (PR #2497 re-cut, 3 of 3).** Reviewing the pass hardened it in several places.
- **A wallet the daily screen finds is never lost.** Once the screen finds a wallet held or confirmed, the wallet stays on a watch list every hour until a fresh read shows nothing left to do. A failed observation therefore never lets it drop out until the next cycle.
- **Observations come first.** Each hour the pass first sends one observation for every wallet due one, held or confirmed. A wallet is due when its last observation is five hours old, so each lands inside the six-hour bound. Wallets whose evidence chain is already running go first, nearest deadline first; a first observation has no bound to miss, so newly found wallets cannot take the budget a running chain needs. Only then does it commit clean results, and only after that does it work through settlement and staging backlogs. Those backlogs are capped per wallet and rotated, so one wallet cannot take the whole budget.
- **A clean result is committed.** A wallet whose old evidence is still stored, and whom the oracle now answers clean, gets that clean result recorded on-chain. A later flag then starts a new hold instead of continuing the old one.
- **Each run fits inside its hour.** Chains run side by side within the run's deadline, and the run stops reading and sending before it, leaving the rest for the next hour.
- **Alerts report what actually happened.** They count observations and clean commits that did not land, not a prediction, and each kind gets its own alert. An observation counts as landed only when a fresh read shows the wallet's evidence actually moved, and no observation is sent while the sanctions oracle is not answering, since it would record nothing. A wallet whose status could not be read, in the candidate list or the daily screen, keeps the capacity alert raised and is named as unread, so a run that could not look never reports everything as covered. A confirmed wallet whose settlement reverts for any reason other than having nothing left to settle gets an alert naming the error, and so does a run whose write budget or time ran out before it reached every wallet with a final verdict. A wallet with a final verdict whose staging record will not resolve gets its own alert, measured from the resolution pages that reverted. It clears when the record resolves. A clean wallet the screen finds still carrying stored sanctions state stays watched until that clean result is committed. A failure in the daily screen or the watch-list bookkeeping costs only that step, never the due observations. A chain with more loan parties than the screen can cover in a day gets an alert too. Operator alerts carry their chain, so a redeploy clears their repeat-suppression for that chain. An alert about a chain is also held to the keeper's epoch fence: once the indexer has moved to a new Diamond, an alert computed from the old one is not posted.

The pass ships disarmed behind `REWARD_SANCTIONS_KEEPER_ENABLED`, and its operator alerts need the ops bot's `TG_OPS_BOT_TOKEN` and `TG_OPS_CHAT_ID`.

## Thread — the delivery-proof and late-acknowledgement passes, on an open-work queue (PR #2497 re-cut, 2 of 3)

**Two passes land.** The delivery-proof pass indexes each mirror delivery's epoch days and proves the day list of each delivery that holds no epoch, so a mirror's funded days stop being gated. The acknowledgement pass now also acknowledges a released remittance whose delivery landed after all, so the canonical chain resolves the share it had counted as stranded. Both ship disarmed: the delivery-proof pass behind its own flag, and the late acknowledgements behind the existing remittance flag.

**Work is kept until the chain says it is done.** Both passes find their work in the shared event log. Each walks the log forward a page at a time, never past what the indexer has fully recorded, and keeps every item still open in a private queue. An item leaves the queue only when the chain shows it finished, never because it aged out of a window or a count. An item that can never finish takes one row and holds nothing else back, and neither read grows with history. Earlier versions read only the last 45 days of deliveries, or the newest 50 releases, so older work could drop out unseen.

**Every run stays inside its own slot.** Each pass now gets a deadline: its scheduled minute plus its own cadence. Both passes reach the chain only through clients that refuse every read once that deadline has passed, and neither starts a send after it, so whatever is left waits for the next run. The delivery-proof pass handles its chains side by side. A failed batch read in either pass's work queue costs one request and leaves its items for the next run, instead of falling back to one request per item. An item that could not be read keeps its place in the rotation, and an unread on-chain state is never taken as "not done". Items take their turn by when they were last tried, so a few old ones cannot hold back newer ones. Ordinary pending acknowledgements go before the late-delivery backlog. A failure in the late step does not stop that tick's ordinary acknowledgements.

**A packet's reservation is read where the packet says it came from.** The delivery-proof pass reads a packet's reservation on the chain and contract the packet itself names as its sender, not on whichever Diamond is configured today. A wrong source cannot pass anyway, because the rebuilt list must match the packet's own root. A send waiting its turn behind another job's send is also refused once the run's deadline passes, at the moment its turn comes and before it is signed. A delivery whose epoch the contract can still admit, because it landed before custody was active, is admitted first and then indexed, never proven as if it had no epoch and dropped. A released remittance already acknowledged, whether its delivery was consumed or quarantined, is no longer acknowledged again on every retry window. Refs #2496, #1944, #1461.

## Thread — the keeper's private database on the always-on machine, and the machine as the keeper's only runtime (PR #2497 re-cut)

**The private database on the machine.** The always-on machine reaches the keeper's private database through the same Cloudflare D1 interface and credentials it already uses for the shared one. The database id is fixed in the code, so a wrong setting cannot point the machine at another database. A Worker deploy applies the private migrations and refuses to publish until the one-time copy is recorded. The machine has no deploy step, so it asks the same two questions before every tick. Does the private database record every keeper migration? Does the copy's record still match the old shared tables? Until both answers are yes, the tick does nothing and logs why. The full check runs on every tick, so a keeper that writes the old tables later stops the machine on its next tick. The machine never applies migrations or copies tables itself; both stay with the keeper's deploy and its copy command. The runbook gives the order: pause the machine's updates, stop the keeper, migrate, copy, resume.

**A redeploy resets the keeper's own state.** When a chain gets a fresh Diamond, its loan numbers and day records start again. A row the keeper kept from the retired Diamond could make a reused loan number look already alerted, or a day look already reported. So the keeper now remembers which Diamond each chain's private state belongs to, as the indexer does for its tables. When a chain's Diamond changes, the keeper clears that chain's rows from every private table that is not already keyed by Diamond. It does this only once the indexer has adopted the same Diamond — and "adopted" means the indexer has finished its own reset for that chain, not merely written the new Diamond down — so the keeper never reads the new Diamond's numbers against the old one's shared rows. The one-time copy writes the keeper's first record from that same rule, and refuses to complete for a chain the indexer has not settled on; a keeper that finds private rows for a chain with no record stops rather than guessing which Diamond they belong to. It deletes, checks each table is empty, and only then records the new Diamond, so an interrupted reset is simply done again. A failed read stops the tick rather than guessing.

**The machine is the keeper's only runtime.** The keeper's Cloudflare Worker no longer runs keeper work on a schedule. A scheduled run that arrives anyway, for instance from a schedule restored by hand, does nothing and says why. The Worker stays deployed only as the private database's migration applier.

**One slow pass no longer holds back the others.** On the machine, a pass that is still running when its next minute comes is not started again, but every other pass due that minute runs on time. Before, the whole minute was skipped, so one slow pass could delay the liquidator. Sends from different passes stay in order because they all go through the keeper's per-account send queue. The machine also renews the runner lease itself, every minute that any pass is still running, even when that minute's tick is declined. So a run of declined ticks no longer lets the lease lapse under a long pass; a renewal that fails is logged and tried again the next minute.

**While a redeploy is being adopted, the keeper waits on every chain.** A chain runs only while the indexer has settled on the same Diamond the keeper is configured with, and the keeper checks that on every tick, not only when its own record changes. So while the indexer has not yet adopted a chain's new Diamond, is still clearing the old one's rows, or has moved to a Diamond the keeper's build does not name yet, every tick declines with a line saying it is waiting, for all chains, liquidations included. That gap normally lasts only until the indexer's next run after its own deploy, or the keeper's next update. A decline that persists means one of the two has not picked up the redeploy.

**A pass already running when the indexer moves stops acting too.** A pass can run past the minute that launched it, and the indexer can adopt a new Diamond in that time. From then on the pass holds the old deployment's view of the chain while the shared records belong to the new one, so anything it did next — a liquidation, an alert, a stored notification — could land on a loan number the new Diamond has given to someone else. The keeper now checks the indexer's adoption at the moment of every effect, in whichever pass. Before it signs a transaction and before it sends an alert, it reads the adoption again and refuses if the indexer has moved. A record it writes to the shared database carries that condition inside the write itself, so the check and the write are one operation that cannot be split by an adoption landing between them; a write that finds the indexer moved changes nothing and is reported as refused. Reading, which changes nothing, is not checked, and the keeper's own private records need no check because its redeploy reset clears them before the new Diamond's first pass. Refs #2496.

## Thread — six deploy-tooling gaps from the end-of-programme redeploys, closed (#2548)

The fresh redeploy of Base Sepolia and Arbitrum Sepolia on 2026-10-17 and of BNB testnet on 2026-10-18 hit six gaps in the deploy scripts. Each was worked around by hand at the time. This change closes them so the next fresh deploy needs none of those manual steps.

**The post-deploy sentinel checks verify something again.** The verify and health steps of all three deploy scripts read two counters that no facet exposes any more. Every run printed a question mark for them and passed. The reads now live in one shared list that all three scripts use, and they name the counters the Diamond actually serves (active offers and active loans). A read that cannot be answered now fails the step instead of printing a question mark. That covers a Diamond that is not cut, not deployed, or not on the chain the RPC serves.

**A killed deploy can no longer leave a fake retired Diamond in the record.** A deploy run's simulation writes the chain's deployment file before anything is broadcast. Killing the run between the two leaves a file naming addresses that were never deployed. The next fresh deploy used to record that address as a retired Diamond in the committed archive inventory, and since the indexer's deployment epochs the indexer takes its retired Diamonds from that inventory. The scripts now check that the recorded Diamond has code on the chain before recording it. If it has none, they stop, record nothing, and print the recovery steps. An RPC that cannot answer is also a refusal, because the record must not claim a Diamond it cannot prove exists.

**The CCIP lane list can live in the operator's settings file.** The list of chains a chain is wired to differs per chain: the hub lists every spoke, and each spoke lists the hub. Until now it could only be typed on the command line for each wiring run. It now resolves per chain, the way the router and the token registries already do. A value typed for one run still works as an override. If the per-chain setting and the typed value disagree, the run is refused rather than guessing which topology was meant.

**The faucet mock rig and the oracle configuration no longer overwrite each other.** On the Base and Arbitrum testnets, the only workable liquidation route is the faucet mock rig's swap adapter, because neither chain has a usable real venue. The rig and the oracle configuration step both set the oracle's feed registry, factory, ETH price feed and WETH, so the rig had to run a second time after configuration to restore its settings. There is now a dedicated testnet-mocks phase that runs before configuration. The configuration step adopts every setting the rig writes instead of the chain's real ones: its feed registry, factory, ETH price feed, WETH, the two price denominators and its legacy 0x proxy pair. Leaving any one of them to the operator settings would have overwritten it, or, for the 0x pair, made the step refuse these chains outright. It does that only on a testnet, and only after confirming against the chain that the recorded factory really holds the rig's pool for the recorded WETH, so an inconsistent record is refused rather than half-applied. One pass is now enough. Chains with a real venue, such as BNB testnet with PancakeSwap, are unaffected.

**A failed facet count no longer passes verify.** The facet-count check in all three scripts printed "all 0 expected facets registered" and passed when its single read of the Diamond's facet list failed, which happened once on BNB testnet. The failed count produced two values instead of one, so the comparison errored instead of failing. The count now comes from one shared helper that returns a single number or refuses, and a refusal fails the step.

**The gas price can be pinned per chain, and is on BNB testnet.** Forge estimated a 1-wei gas price for one BNB testnet transaction, below the node's minimum, so the transaction never mined and the deploy hung until it was replaced by hand. Each chain can now carry a pinned gas price in the operator settings, BNB testnet defaults to 1 gwei when none is set, and a price given for the run still takes precedence.

Closes #2548.

## Thread — the first perk with an effect: a priority credit lets a refused intent fill through (#2550, #2559)

The spend-gated perk channel could sell entitlements but nothing used one, so no perk could responsibly be priced. The first effect is now built: priority routing for lender intents. A lender who buys credits and opts one of their live intents in gets fills that would otherwise have been refused. When a solver's service capacity is used up, a fill of that intent still goes through, spending one of the lender's credits instead of the solver's capacity. When the solver can afford the fill, it is charged as usual and no credit is spent, so a credit only ever buys a fill that would not have happened. Nobody else is reordered or charged more, which keeps the rule that a perk may change what its buyer gets but never what other participants see.

The opt-in belongs to one specific intent. Rewriting the intent resets it and cancelling clears it, so a replacement never silently spends the lender's credits, and every change is recorded. Intents run by the protocol itself, the aggregator adapters and the backstop vault, cannot opt in. The perk cannot be sold in a form it cannot deliver. It can only be priced as counted credits, and it cannot be bought unless it is configured that way, the solver capacity limit is on and lender-intent matching is switched on. Taking its price down is always allowed, so a deployment that had configured it as a timed perk before it gained its effect can still withdraw that offer, and such a timed configuration is never sold again. The effect switches on only when the operator prices the perk with this version, once and with a recorded event. A price or credits left over from before the effect existed never activate on their own, and a deployment that had already sold this perk cannot switch it on for priority routing at all. That check is made at the purchase, so a later change that switches either off stops sales at once. A new read shows a solver, as of the current block, whether its own capacity covers a fill or the lender's credit would.

Nothing is priced. The perk stays unsold until three things are in place on a chain: the solver capacity limit is on, the keeper has been updated so it keeps looking for credit-backed intents after a capacity refusal, and the effect has been verified on a fork. The app gains its screens when lender intents get a user interface.

Closes #2559. Refs #2550.
